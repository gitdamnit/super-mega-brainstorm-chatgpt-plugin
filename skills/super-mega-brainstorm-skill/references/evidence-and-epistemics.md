# Evidence, causality, pseudoscience and model self-deception

## Truth-status discipline

Use `OBSERVED`, `VERIFIED`, `SUPPORTED (strength given)`, `PLAUSIBLE`, `SPECULATIVE`, `UNKNOWN`, `CONTRADICTED`, `FALSE`, and `VALUE JUDGMENT`. Each status needs scope and rationale. Unknown is not false; unexplained is not evidence for a favored explanation. Source titles/URLs are not proof that the content was read.

## Evidence chain (highest-priority claims)

**Claim → operational definition → source/data → date and applicability → study or measurement quality → alternatives/confounds → synthesis → wording calibrated to evidence.** For a time-sensitive claim check current primary records. Contrast primary, replication, systematic review, expert analysis, witness report, marketing copy, AI-generated claim. No single hierarchy applies to every field. Don't invent a consensus.

## Causal reasoning tripwires

1. Temporal order: cause should ordinarily precede effect, but timing alone does not show cause.
2. Confounders: could a third variable produce both observation and outcome?
3. Reverse direction: could outcome cause supposed cause?
4. Selection/collider bias: were subjects selected in a way that creates spurious association?
5. Regression to mean: did an extreme measurement tend naturally toward typical?
6. Mechanism: does a proposed pathway make coherent, testable predictions?
7. Counterfactual: what would likely have happened absent intervention?
8. Dose/response and replication: when relevant, is the relationship consistent and reproducible?
9. Extrapolation: do group-to-individual and study-to-population transitions hold?
10. Interventions and ethical designs: what design best discriminates causation from correlation?

## Statistical/measurement tripwires

Denominator omitted; small/unrepresentative sample; survivorship; p-value as probability the null is true; significance as size or importance; confidence intervals misunderstood; multiple comparisons without correction; flexible stopping; HARKing; publication bias; base-rate neglect; percent-change without absolute baseline; Simpson's paradox; ecological/individual inference; measurement drift; overfitting; leakage; out-of-sample collapse; fragile subgroups; missing data mechanism; inappropriate models; overprecision; unit mismatch; selection on outcome; unreported uncertainty. For each, say **what is actually known**, effect on claim, and a fix. Don't pretend to diagnose statistical defects without underlying design/data.

## Pseudoscience claim audit

Ask five operational questions:
1. What precise, observable prediction differentiates claim X from rivals?
2. Is there a conceivable result that would materially lower confidence in X?
3. Are methods disclosed enough for independent scrutiny or replication when appropriate?
4. Are failed predictions addressed with independently testable revisions or limitless ad hoc exceptions?
5. Are sources, effects and mechanisms proportionate to claims, and have negative findings been considered?

**Do not treat these as a mechanical science/non-science boundary:** philosophical accounts of scientific method differ; qualitative inquiry, historical explanation, ethics, and early exploratory science require fit-for-purpose assessment. Report **unsupported scientific claim** or **untestable as stated**, not blanket “fraud” without evidence.

## Bias versus fallacy

Bias is a tendency in cognition/data collection and may *cause* an error; a fallacy is a defect in an actual argument. Common biases to self-check: confirmation, anchoring, availability, survivorship, framing, hindsight, outcome bias, sunk cost, loss aversion, planning fallacy, halo/horns, groupthink, motivated reasoning, status quo, optimism, pessimism, automation bias, authority overtrust, and selection bias. Flag **risk of bias** unless actual distorted inference is evidenced.

## Adversarial model self-audit

- Restate the user's favorite claim as if it belonged to an unknown opponent; re-evaluate it.
- Ask for *a serious case against your own recommendation* and an alternative explanation consistent with observed facts.
- Check invented citations, invented agent reports, unperformed calculations, source-date mistakes, fabricated consensus, bogus specificity, falsely precise scores, unstated premise shifts, and absent assumptions.
- Compare claim wording to source strength. “Proves,” “always,” “will,” “caused,” “scientifically established,” and “everyone” require unusually strong support.
- Check if argument depends on equivocal definitions; lock terms before formalization.
- Check for circular sourcing: multiple sites repeating one unsupported original are **one lineage**, not independent confirmation.
- Perform a final **could I be wrong?** checkpoint: list what would change this conclusion and how to find out.

## Claim severity and reporting

`BLOCKER` = decisive invalid inference, false premise or unsupported high-impact assertion; must repair or withhold conclusion.
`MAJOR` = material weakness that could change recommendation; label and test.
`CAUTION` = important but not decisive uncertainty; qualify.
`CLEAR` = no identified error at performed level; **never** a truth certificate.
`UNVERIFIABLE` = evidence not accessible; annotate separately from severity.

## Reporting example

Claim: “Five friends liked a mockup, so 90% of the market will buy.” **MAJOR: hasty/unrepresentative sampling and unjustified extrapolation.** Five acquaintances provide concept feedback, not a market purchase rate. Repair: “Early reactions were positive among five acquaintances; demand remains unknown.” Test on target buyers with pre-set purchase-proxy and sampling criteria.
